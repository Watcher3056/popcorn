# Craft: story, picture, pace

Directions for what the viewer sees and hears. Each names what it protects; most come with examples and anti-examples. The examples are from varied products on purpose: they show the kind of thinking, not scenes to reuse.

## The piece

- [CR-KIND] **A staged picture never passes for the real thing.** Protects: trust; a viewer who spots a staged picture inside "the real thing" doubts the rest.
  - **Marketing** sells the idea: abstract, simplified pictures of the product and the pain, or real footage, with a few large words, like an ad.
  - **A real presentation** shows the product as it is (a recording of it, its real interface, the object itself, etc.), with no claims over it that the footage doesn't show.
  - Where both kinds sit on one page (a README, a landing page, etc.), each is labelled for what it is ([VID-HONEST]).
  - Examples: a budgeting app's page opens with a drawn loop (coins leaking out of a jar, then the jar sealed), and its "How it works" section shows a screen recording of the actual app; a game's trailer cuts real gameplay with "Out on Friday".
  - Anti-example: a drawn mock-up of an app's screen captioned as a recording; a real recording with claims pasted over it that it doesn't show ("2× faster" over a clip that shows no timing).
- [CR-STORY] **A story with a beginning, not a list of features.** Protects: attention; a list of features reads as "one more tool", a story of a problem someone had and solved reads as worth watching.
  - A shape that worked for a narrated piece (a direction, not a template): the hook → who is speaking and why → what is coming (the problems, up front) → each problem → the turn (where the story changes direction: the problem gives way to the answer) → the answers, in the same order as the problems → how the parts fit together → what the viewer gets → what to do next. The ending can echo the opening picture, now resolved.
  - **The first seconds carry the hook** (the pain or the promise), even in a story; the list of what is coming follows it. A strong hook is often the running example played out as events the viewer has lived (a bus pulling away as they reach the stop, a form that loses everything on submit, a boss fight lost on its last hit, etc.), then one short line where they recognise themselves, then the pain said as theirs too ("we", "you"), not only the speaker's. A greeting or an introduction before the hook reads as a generic intro, and the viewer scrolls on.
  - **Say what is coming before going through it.** A short list of the problems lets the viewer follow, and returning to that list between parts shows where they are (an item turns done, the next lights up). Starting straight with problem, answer, problem, answer leaves them guessing how long it goes on.
  - **Each scene follows from the one before,** as cause → effect, so the viewer never wonders why they are seeing it.
  - Challenge: can the viewer say at any moment where they are in the story? If not, return to the list between parts. Would a scene make sense if it were moved elsewhere? Then tie it to the scene before as its effect, or cut it.
  - Anti-example: "Feature 1 · Feature 2 · Feature 3 · Download now"; a logo sting followed straight by a tour of the menus (a tutorial is the exception: there the tour is the point).
- [CR-ONE-CASE] **One running example carries the whole piece.** Protects: clarity; every new example costs the viewer a new setup to understand.
  - Pick one concrete case and show every problem and every answer on it. A second domain appears only when the point is "it works beyond this case", and then it is shown as exactly that.
  - Before a draft goes out, search the scenes' text for leftovers of an earlier example.
  - Example: a fitness tracker's video follows one runner's week through every scene: the missed goal, the plan, the result.
  - Anti-example: the problem shown on a running app, the answer on a cycling app, and the proof on a sleep chart.

## The picture

- [CR-SHOW] **Show it happen; don't caption it.** Protects: understanding without reading. The test: listening to the narration and without reading the words on screen, does the scene make sense, like a cartoon? Where the piece plays muted (a feed, an autoplay hook, etc.), the picture alone carries it.
  - A failure is an event the viewer watches: the thing breaks, falls, leaks, runs late. Success is the same event going right.
  - Example: instead of "the export fails silently", the progress bar reaches the end, the file icon appears, and opening it shows an empty page with a red ✕. In a delivery tracker: the van icon stops at the wrong house. For a bike lock: the cutter slips off the shackle.
  - A familiar exchange (a support chat, a group chat, a comment thread, etc.) shown as its own bubbles is recognised faster than a symbol standing for it. Example: a dinner-planning app's pain as the group chat itself: "Where do we eat?" → "Anything's fine" → "Not pizza" → "So… where?".
  - Anti-example: a large line of text in the middle of the screen naming the problem ("Sync is slow", "Saves are lost"); two halves of a comparison that look the same and differ only in their captions.
- [CR-ONE-ACTION] **One action per screen, and a chain of actions as a chain of screens.** Protects: following the cause → effect.
  - When a beat is "you do X → it does Y → Z happens", show X, then Y, then Z, each on its own screen or as one continuous move, joined by quick, seamless transitions that keep the viewer watching.
  - Example: "You ask for a refund" → the form sends → three days pass on a calendar → the balance is unchanged, red ✕.
  - Anti-example: all three steps drawn at once in one frame; two detached pictures side by side (a diagram above, a result below) with nothing showing how one leads to the other.
- [CR-CALM] **Little on screen at once.** Protects: readability; a frame where many things appear and the screen keeps changing reads as noise.
  - A few elements per frame, a calm camera, one focus at a time.
  - In a marketing piece, simplify the interface to the parts that tell the story: grey bars for text, blocks for panels, etc. In a real presentation the product stays as it is ([CR-KIND]); the calm comes from framing: a zoom on the part in use, one action at a time, etc.
  - Example: a sales dashboard shown as its one chart and its one number; a recording of a photo editor zoomed on the slider being dragged.
  - Anti-example: a project board shown in full, with every card, filter and avatar moving at once and the view panning every second.
- [CR-MARK] **Mark bad and good with a clear sign, not with a mood.** Protects: the picture; filters and shaking make the whole scene worse to look at.
  - A red ✕ at the bad thing, a green ✓ at the good one or a light outline around it. An outline or a soft highlight draws the eye; darkening everything but one spot hides the context.
  - **Nothing covers the subject:** a mark, a label or a badge sits at the edge of the thing's picture (a round ✕ in its corner, etc.) or as one line under it, so the thing itself stays whole.
  - Example: a delivery app's wrong stop gets a ✕ at its pin's corner, the right one a ✓; a photo of a cracked phone screen gets its ✕ in the photo's corner and "cracked after one drop" under it.
  - Anti-example: a red tint over the whole frame to say "bad"; a vignette that darkens all but one button; a camera that shakes for seconds; a label patch laid over the crack itself.
- [CR-PROOF] **Show the proof, not only the claim.** Protects: belief; "it's solved" is a claim, a visible before → after is evidence.
  - Wherever the answer is "it works now", show what shows it: a before → after the viewer can compare (a wipe across the same frame), a value against its target, the evidence next to the conclusion, etc.
  - Example: a photo editor's noise fix as a slider wiping across the same photo; a thermostat's savings as this month's bar under last month's.
  - Anti-example: a green "Done ✓" card and nothing to show what was done.
- [CR-PARTS] **Parts of a whole as a casual picture, not an architecture diagram.** Protects: approachability; a labelled block diagram reads like documentation.
  - When the product is made of parts (a core and its add-ons, an app and its plug-ins, etc.), show them as pieces that fit together, plug in or click into place, each named once, with room for the viewer's own piece where that is true.
  - When pieces combine into one result, show the combining as an event (ingredients drop into one bowl, single notes join into one chord, etc.), so the viewer sees what it is made of.
  - Example: a camera kit: body, lens and flash click together, and an empty mount shows where the viewer's own lens fits.
  - Anti-example: an online shop's platform drawn as boxes "Core / Plugins / Themes" joined by arrows, as in its docs.
- [CR-STRIKING] **Striking comes from a visible change, and structure comes before effects.** Protects: the difference between "presented well" and "remembered".
  - A piece reads as average when it is even all the way through: the same pace, the same look, a title card for every point. What lands is a moment of change: before → the change in one move → after. Example: scattered sticky notes fly together and become one ordered board.
  - Contrast helps the change land (cramped → open, many steps → one), and so does an ending that resolves the opening picture.
  - Effects never replace structure: a version with more effects and a weaker order of scenes reads as chaotic. When the user prefers an earlier, plainer version, keep its structure and bring over only the moments that work.
- [CR-VARIETY] **No two scenes tell their point with the same picture.** Protects: attention; scenes that look almost the same feel like a loop.
  - Each problem and each answer gets its own visual idea, in the piece's one style ([CR-FINISH]). A scene that would only repeat the previous picture with a new label continues the previous scene instead ([CR-TRANSITIONS]).
  - Example: a travel app's three pains as three pictures: a ticket blowing away, a gate number flipping, a price tag growing.
  - Anti-example: the same three pains, each shown as the same phone screen with a different red banner.

## Time

- [CR-PACE] **Fast enough to keep the viewer watching, slow enough for each reveal to land.** Protects: the viewer staying to the end.
  - No long pauses and no dead air; trim waits between beats.
  - A reveal holds long enough to be seen before the next thing starts: a status that changes, a "Done" that turns out to be broken, a question before its answer. The draft is where these holds are judged; the user's "too fast here" or "it drags there" is fixed at that spot.
  - A long scroll or a long block of text runs shorter: cut its length rather than speeding it up.
- [CR-TRANSITIONS] **Scene changes are seamless, and the scene changes only where the picture really changes.** Protects: polish; a glitch at a scene change is what viewers notice first.
  - Nothing of the previous or the next scene shows through a scene change: a scene starts after its page's earlier beat has left, and its last frames hold its own picture.
  - When two beats share the same picture, don't change the scene: change only what differs (a title swaps, a panel slides in over the same view).
  - A thin new slide that only adds one fact usually works better as the continuation of the scene before (the same view scrolls on, the same window shows the next message, etc.).
  - When one text replaces another in the same place, the old one is gone before the new one comes; they never overlap.
  - An opening flows into what follows (the title's logo moves up into a header and the first scene builds under it) rather than snapping.
  - Check: [VID-LOOK].

## Words

- [CR-WORDS] **Words on screen are few, short and in the viewer's words.** Protects: reading at a glance.
  - A few large words, like an ad: a label, a promise, a number with its unit. No technical noise in a marketing piece (logs, file paths, internal names, etc.) unless the noise itself is the point being shown.
  - A label names what the viewer will recognise, and doesn't blur the point it stands for. Example: a password manager's problem "the same password everywhere", labelled "Security?", reads as a problem about hackers; "One password, reused everywhere" says what is meant.
  - Where the piece plays muted, its spoken lines also appear as subtitles ([CR-FEED]).
  - The narration is plain and casual, in short sentences: in the first person when a presenter speaks, otherwise straight to the viewer. The one thesis line comes at the end.
  - An opening that could belong to any product ("The smart way to work") is replaced by what this audience recognises at once (its own concrete pain, the product's own moment, etc.).

## The presenter and the ending

- [CR-PRESENTER] **When a person stands behind the product, let them open it:** the hook in their own words, or right after it ([CR-STORY]); then their photo, name and role, and straight into what is coming. Protects: trust; a face with a name and a role reads as "someone built this to fix their own problem".
  - In a piece with no voice that is read in order (a carousel, a slideshow, etc.), the person comes last instead, as the sign-off ([CR-CAROUSEL]); a still carries the face itself ([CR-THUMB]).
  - The voice follows [VID-VOICE-ID].
  - The ending says what to do next: where to try it, where to follow, with short links. Where viewers watch on another screen than the phone they'd scan with (a desktop, a TV, a talk, etc.), add a QR code that has been decoded back to the exact link.

## Short loops (GIF, autoplay hooks)

- [CR-CLIP] **One hook, one visible change, one payoff, in one loop.** Protects: the few seconds a loop gets on a page.
  - Opens with what the viewer recognises at once (a pain the product removes, the moment a game is about, etc.), shows the change as an event, ends on the payoff (a one-line promise in a marketing loop; the result itself in a real one), and loops back without a jump.
  - Smooth: motion at a frame rate where it doesn't step; check the loop at its real speed. Keep it inside the page's size budget; light and dark themes need either both versions or one that reads on both.

## Thumbnails and covers

- [CR-THUMB] **The click comes from one picture read in a second.** Protects: the viewer choosing to press play.
  - Made for itself: composed for the thumbnail, even when it starts from a real frame, rather than a frame grabbed as it is. The picture is the hook (the pain or its punchline, the moment a game is about, etc.; [CR-CLIP]); the headline, in the audience's words, says what the piece is about or what the viewer gets, and doesn't repeat what the picture already says. The brand or the call goes smaller under it, unless the name itself is the draw (a known series, a famous brand, etc.); a carousel's cover leaves them to the next slides ([CR-CAROUSEL]). Example: "Receipts that file themselves" with "Shoebox" under it. Anti-example: "Shoebox 2.0 is here!" alone, which says nothing to someone who hasn't heard of it.
  - **It carries the main idea:** a viewer who sees only the cover can say what it offers (what the product is for, what the game is, what the talk teaches, etc.). A gag about the pain alone sells the gag, not the product.
  - **The product's own art can lead** (its footage, its art, its photos, etc.) where the audience already knows its world (a game's players, a brand's customers, etc.): it reads as theirs. Elsewhere a drawn picture serves as well, as long as it reads at the size.
  - Where there are several ideas, show them side by side at feed size, so the user picks by looking.
  - The eye lands on the subject at once ([CR-MARK]).
  - When it compares two states, the two halves differ visibly in the picture, not only in their captions.
  - A face, when a person stands behind it.
  - Check it at the size of the feed and of a phone: the subject, the headline and the face still read (`scripts/feedsize.py`).

## Feeds and carousels

- [CR-ONE-MESSAGE] **One piece, one promise.** Protects: the viewer's picture of what it is about; an opening that promises one thing and parts that deliver another read as two stories at once.
  - The opening (the first seconds, the cover, the first slide) promises what the piece delivers, and every part keeps it. Where the content carries more than one message (two products, two audiences, a core and its add-ons, etc.), the opening promises the whole and each part says which of them it is about ([CR-PARTS]); an opening about one part and slides about another break the promise. When the user would rather split it into pieces, that is theirs to choose.
  - The opening's key phrase comes back where the piece explains it, so the headline doesn't stay a riddle.
  - A narrator can carry the viewer across a turn the pictures alone don't explain; a carousel or a still has no voice, so one made from a video is checked again against its one promise.
  - Example: a fitness app's carousel opens on "A workout that fits your lunch break", and every slide is a workout that does.
  - Anti-example: a cover "Leftovers into dinner" followed by slides about the same app's table booking.
- [CR-FEED] **In a feed, the first frame and the first seconds decide, mostly without sound.** Protects: the stop; a feed plays a piece muted for a moment as people scroll past, and most decide there. A view count shows that reach, not interest; the watch-time numbers show where people left ([VID-ROUND]).
  - **Frame 0 is the hook picture, complete:** it is what the feed and the player show before play. The same picture goes into the file as its cover and to the platform as its custom thumbnail ([CR-THUMB]). Example: a bike lock's clip opens on the cutter already on the shackle. Anti-example: a slow fade in from black, or a logo before the picture.
  - **Subtitles carry the narration for the muted viewer,** made from the script (or from a transcript checked against the names), so names are spelled right. Burned in, they cover nothing the viewer needs (a strip of their own, a calm part of the frame, etc.) and stay clear of what the platform lays over the frame ([CR-FINISH]); the platform's automatic subtitles then stay off, or they would show twice. Otherwise the subtitles file goes up with the video.
  - Challenge: paused on frame 0, muted, at feed size, does a stranger know what this is about and why to keep watching? If not, make frame 0 the hook's most telling moment ([CR-THUMB]) and check that the subtitles carry the opening line.
- [CR-CAROUSEL] **A carousel: the cover hooks, the next slide pays off, each slide earns the next swipe.** Protects: the swipe; with no voice and no motion, each slide has to work alone.
  - **The cover is the hook only** ([CR-THUMB]): the headline in the audience's words over one picture. The brand, the call, kickers (the small line above a headline), labels, feature lists, etc. wait for the next slides.
  - **The payoff comes at once:** many readers swipe only a slide or two, so the second slide delivers what the cover promised (what the product does, the first step of a how-to, the answer to the cover's question, etc.), with the list of what is coming where several parts follow ([CR-STORY]). The rest follows in the order the piece needs (the problems and their answers, the steps, the tips, etc.), on one running example where there is one ([CR-ONE-CASE]).
  - **As few slides as the story needs,** one idea each; a slide that only announces the next ones, or repeats one, goes.
  - **It reads at a phone's width,** including the words inside a screenshot: zoom to the part that matters, or put its outcome in one large line under it ([CR-MARK]).
  - **It shows there is more** (a page number, an arrow at the edge, a picture that runs into the next slide, etc.).
  - **The last slide signs off; it doesn't greet:** who made it, where a person or a team stands behind it, with the action (try it, follow, etc.). A greeting or an introduction there reads as a start.
  - Example: a meal kit's carousel: the cover "Dinner, decided." over a full plate; then "Pick three recipes, get one box"; then the three hassles and their answers; last, the link and "Made by …, follow for new menus".
  - Anti-example: a cover with the headline, the product's name, three feature chips and two screenshots; a last slide that opens with a greeting and the maker's name, as if the carousel were only starting.

## The text that goes with it

- [CR-POST] **The post, the description or the README text follows the same story.** Protects: one message; text and video that say different things weaken each other.
  - The first line carries the hook, since feeds cut text after a line or two. Then the same problems and answers as the video, in the same words; the links; a question or an action to end.
  - No numbers or claims the video and the product don't show ([VID-HONEST]).

## Finish

- [CR-FINISH] **Check the finish on frames, at the longest text and the busiest moment.** Protects: quality the viewer notices without knowing why.
  - The style (background, palette, type, etc.) stays the same in every scene; a background texture that disappears in one scene reads as a regression.
  - No wrapped or clipped labels, no overlapping pieces, no glyphs drawn as boxes, clean joints where pieces meet, text inside its cards.
  - Words stay out of the parts of the frame the platform covers (a feed's captions and buttons, a player's controls, etc.).
